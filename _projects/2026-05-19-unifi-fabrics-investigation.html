Overview

This member building ran its doors on Ubiquiti’s UniFi Access. Ubiquiti had sold a technical decision-maker on its Fabrics beta: one console to administrate several sites. The beta came with thin API coverage, shaky reliability, and little support, and many of the building’s problems started there.

In spring 2026 the doors started failing. By late April the doors worked only if someone restarted a server every three hours, day and night. The bugs were Ubiquiti’s, and its updates said little, so we diagnosed them ourselves: five weeks, fifteen bugs, each filed with stack traces. Firmware and a clean reboot had the doors quiet by mid-May.

I set the priorities and coordinated the investigation between Ubiquiti and the operations team. I collated the engineer’s retrospective and the Ubiquiti support case into this report, and designed it so executive and technical leadership could read the same page. Then I went into the data warehouse to price what broken access, and broken member email, had cost.

Where things stood in March

The building, several hundred members across 18 levels, opened on UniFi Access with manual door access, no identity system, and no developer APIs. That bought a year of firefighting. By March 2026 I had ranked access and identity P0, the blocker under everything else on the roadmap. Here is what that meant on the ground.

Doors at the ceiling
The Door Hub Minis on the front door, lab, and gym hit their storage ceiling. We cut access policies from 40 to 30 and set a hard cap of 32, under a ceiling near 36. One policy too many and the door fails silently; members get locked out.
Elevators left open
Two elevator hubs rebooted intermittently between 5 and 9 PM. The workaround was to leave the elevators unlocked, which exposed all 18 levels overnight. A security failure and a UX failure at once.
Identity built, not connected
OpenLDAP held every member and group, and nothing read from it. Fabric had no People Management API, and no member had an LDAP password. Onboarding meant an admin hand-creating each user in the Fabric console: hours to days, with no status sent back to the member.
Failure without feedback
A failed tap told nobody whether the credential, the reader, or the hub was at fault. No status page, no self-service path. Members called or texted whoever they could find, and one or two engineers (not on call around the clock) were the only fix.
Offboarding on faith
No documented SLA. Leavers could keep live wallet credentials after they left.

On 17 April, door access, policy saves, and credential sync collapsed together. The bugs lived in Ubiquiti's own firmware and server code, and at the April peak the doors needed a cred-server bounce every three hours to keep working. This report covers those five weeks.

The UniFi Fabrics investigation

Executive summary

As of 18 May 2026, all chronic high-impact bugs are dormant. The cred-server has held a single PID for five days and one hour, a record across the investigation. The rate-limit storm, the watermill deadlock, and the access-policy panic family have each been silent for more than five straight days, and the forced bounces have stopped.

Two things did it: Ubiquiti firmware bumps shipped during the window (unifi-credential-server through 1.6.119, unifi-access to 4.2.27, unifi-core to 5.1.104) and a clean EFG reboot on 13 May that drained accumulated state. They landed together, so the credit can't be split. What we can say is that this quiet behaves differently from every earlier reboot's relief. It has held a full week without rebound; earlier reboots bought hours, or a few days at best.

One real issue is still open: Bug #11, the face and credential restore gap. 149 user face records are stranded on EFG and don't replicate to the UNVR, so those users re-enroll by hand until Ubiquiti ships the firmware fix. A new, minor panic family (roughly 70 occurrences per day) fires on deactivated users' stale devices. It's cosmetic; we filed it for completeness.

Timeline

Door access, policy saves, and credential sync all degraded the same morning. The app told users to "ensure your device is connected to the internet." Wrong story. The logs told the real one: the cred-server's rate limiter was 429-throttling the UCS-agent's internal sync calls; the timeouts hit assert.NoError sites (a Go pattern that panics on a returned error instead of handling it); those panicked; and the panic-catching middleware returned HTTP 200 with an empty body, swallowing the failure whole. Operators saw a spinner, a timeout, then a network error that pointed nowhere near the cause. The doors were not the doors.

Getting the doors back that day took database-level surgery on the UNVR's watermill queue, then a full EFG reboot. A phantom replay in the iOS Access app's audit log (Bug #9) traced to non-idempotent event emission in the UNVR's ingest path for pre-LDAP orphans; a Fabric-level DELETE for affected users mitigated it.

Each cred-server bounce bought three to six hours before the rate-limit storm rebuilt to user-impacting levels, and each window ran shorter than the last. The team named it "the bounce treadmill." By 27 April the 429 rate had climbed to roughly 1,600 per hour, the watermill was deadlocked at 1,988 pending rows over three and a half days, and the cred-server's json_systemlog id collision (Bug #1) was firing roughly 3,600 times per hour in the background.

Most of the support-case follow-up files (one per investigation day) came out of this phase, and so did the automation: scheduled status reports to the #unifi-fabric Slack channel, a /restart-credserver skill for ad-hoc bounces, and the watermill-surgery and phantom-replay fixes written up as reusable playbooks. The method settled too. SSH-based log review; corrected source-of-truth log paths (the 429s lived in nginx-identity-hub-access.log, not the usual access log); per-cgroup memory accounting on the UNVR.

A user-visible "Unable to send invitation" error turned out to be a new panic site (SendInvitation in cred-server) on the same assert.NoError anti-pattern as April's panic family. Underneath it, all 2,144 rows in the invitation table, going back four months, carried expired_at = 0001-01-01 00:00:00+00: the Go zero value, because the insert path never wrote the field. A read function looked for a row with expired_at > now(), found none, returned an error, and assert.NoError panicked. PanicHandler returned 200 with an empty body; the UI's notification websocket eventually timed out and surfaced the same misleading "internet connection" error.

Filed as Bug #15 on case with full stack trace, data condition, and a verified GET /invitation_link workaround. Same bug class as Bug #12, different binary.

EFG rebooted at 13:31 PDT on 13 May. The chronic bugs went dormant and held a full week with no forced bounce (per-bug numbers in the grid below). Earlier reboots bought a honeymoon. This quiet says the underlying conditions changed too, beyond the local state.

Root causes

The fifteen numbered bugs cluster into four mechanism families. An executive reader doesn't need the engineering detail; the table is here for its shape. One anti-pattern, propagated through four mechanisms, into fifteen surface symptoms.

FamilyBugsMechanism, plain
assert.NoError panic #8, #10, #11, #12, #13, #14, #15 Recoverable errors wrapped in assert.NoError panic instead of returning. Panic-catching middleware returns HTTP 200 with empty body. Caller sees success. Downstream state diverges silently.
Rate-limiter starvation #2 plus secondary chain Per-actor token buckets had no exemption for the internal sync path. After any cred-server downtime, every reconnect 429'd, the agent backed off, and the system never caught up. Throttling cascaded into timeouts at the panic sites above.
Queue without dead-letter #3 The watermill consumer retried failed batches indefinitely. Any non-retryable error deadlocked the cursor. Cleared only by database surgery or a full reboot.
Sync data integrity #1, #4, #6, #9, #11, #15 Write-path bugs leaving fields uninitialized, id-space collisions across services, lost delete tombstones during stuck-watermill windows, and non-idempotent event emission. All visible only as user-side weirdness, never as a clean 4xx or 5xx.

The through-line is silent failure. A clean 4xx or 5xx would have exposed every one of these within hours. Instead they surfaced days or weeks after the real failure: as 200 OK with an empty body, as audit-log replay on the iOS app, as state quietly drifting apart. At the executive level, the five weeks were the work of making silent failure legible.

Deactivated-device panic family
BindPushToken, CredAuthLogin, GetUserCredentialById: every occurrence is on a deactivated user's stale device that already has no access. Same anti-pattern as #12 and #15. No operational impact; filed on case as another call-site list in the panic-anti-pattern family.

Bug #8 sits in the assert.NoError family above, but the source retrospective gives it no status line, so it has no cell here. The grid shows fourteen of the fifteen numbered bugs.

Firmware moved through the window: cred-server 1.6.115 → 1.6.117 → 1.6.119+3357; unifi-access 4.2.16 → 4.2.27+11896; unifi-core 5.1.95 → 5.1.104. Bug #7 (the goroutine leak) was confirmed fixed in 1.6.115, and later versions plausibly account for further reductions. The unifi-access panic sites (#12, #13, #14) are quiet on the current version. EFG rebooted at 13:31 PDT on 13 May; the watermill drained immediately and the bounces stopped completely.

We can't pin the post-13-May quiet on one factor. Earlier firmware bumps in the window didn't produce this much relief, which says the reboot was load-bearing. Earlier reboots bought only hours to days, which says the firmware deltas mattered too. Most likely both. So we call it dormant, not solved.

  • Cred-server uptime trajectory. Five days and one hour is a record. Track whether it crosses seven, fourteen, thirty days without rebound. Any return of Bug #2 429s in the nginx tier would be the leading indicator.
  • The MSR cgroup events_high counter. Currently at 1.54M and incrementing, but zero OOM kills. Watch for any actual kill, or for a behavior change.
  • The cosmetic deactivated-device panic family. Currently no operational impact. Watch for any expansion to live-user call sites.
  • Bug #11 face restore. No movement; faces remain stranded. Affected users currently re-enrolling on demand as a stopgap.
  • EFG /boot/firmware at 100% disk. Cosmetic but blocks future firmware updates until an off-hours cleanup window.

The status report still posts to #unifi-fabric twice daily (07:00 and 19:00 PDT); auto-bounce has been off since 13 May. If a chronic bug regresses, it shows up there within twelve hours.

Apart from the fixes, the investigation left behind operating infrastructure that is now standing procedure. A CEO should know about these, because they make the next incident shorter.

  • Twice-daily scheduled status report to #unifi-fabric, described under monitoring above.
  • An interactive /restart-credserver skill for ad-hoc bounces with pre and post evidence capture and Slack notification. Now retired from routine use; on standby if Bug #2 returns.
  • A watermill surgery procedure (reference_watermill_surgery.md): pg_dump, cursor advance, DELETE. Validated across multiple runs. Reusable.
  • A phantom-replay Fabric DELETE workaround (reference_phantom_replay_fix.md) for pre-LDAP orphans on the iOS audit log path.
  • The bug-class taxonomy in the table above, which makes each new panic site predictable. The cosmetic deactivated-device family took minutes to diagnose because the pattern was already known.
  • Memory-backed investigation continuity. Every session resumes with full bug history, log paths, methodology, and operator preferences without re-discovery, across shifts, weeks, and vendors.

Next steps

  • File the deactivated-user panic call sites on case as an addendum to the existing assert.NoError anti-pattern asks.
  • Press Ubiquiti on Bug #11 (face restore). This is the only user-impacting issue still open.
  • Defer cred-server bounces indefinitely unless Bug #2 returns. The treadmill has stopped; don't bounce prophylactically.
  • Plan an off-hours /boot/firmware cleanup on EFG so the next firmware update is not gated on disk space.
  • Hold the current monitoring posture through at least one more full firmware release cycle to confirm the remission is not version-specific to 1.6.119 / 4.2.27 / 5.1.104.

The investigation made the access-and-identity pipe legible. The next chapter prices it: what the pipe is worth when it works and when it doesn't, with the BI warehouse as the measuring instrument.

A door reader that does not open, an approval email that never arrives, a renewal reminder blocked at the gateway: the BI data puts a cost on each.

Quantified costs

A member who can't get into the building is a churn risk. A member email that never arrives is a revenue risk. The BI warehouse measures both, and shows what the UniFi investigation was protecting in dollar terms.

100%Thread I · The base at risk

Every member depends on the door

The client's revenue is monthly member subscriptions. Every member in that base has an access credential and an expectation that the door opens on arrival. The UniFi outage on 17 April reached that population within hours. Had it run at peak intensity, the losses would have come as a cohort, not one cancellation a month.

Read forward: the investigation retired a five-week treadmill that cost operator time, member trust, and on-call hours every week it ran. That is what was at stake.

17,449Thread II · The comms pipe

Member email failed silently for two months

Between February and March 2026, 17,449 transactional emails were refused at the destination or blocked at the sender. Newsletter opens fell from roughly 75% to 53% in lockstep. That sender carries renewal reminders, password resets, payment-failure notices AND access-support email. When the cred-server rate-limited the UCS-agent, members felt it within hours, because the doors stopped opening. When the email pipe broke for two months, nobody saw it until April's cancellations.

1 in 3Thread III · Cost of comms failure

One in three cancellations was a failed payment

Over the last twelve months, 177 cancellations were driven by payment_failed in Stripe. These are members whose card declined and whose recovery emails never reached them. That is one in three of all cancellations, given up by the billing pipeline rather than the product. At observed retention, each prevented involuntary churn is worth about six months of a member's fees. That is the dollar value of "the email reaches the member."

Read sideways: the same observation applies to access support. A ticket that goes unanswered for three days, a password reset that gets blocked, an approval email that never lands: each one has a measurable cost in the same units.

107Thread IV · April's arithmetic

April’s cancellation spike started in February

April 2026 logged 107 cancellations against a trailing-twelve-month mean of forty-five. The arithmetic is direct: cards declined in February, retry emails were blocked by the deliverability incident, members never updated their card, second retry declined in March, auto-cancellation fired in April. Same structure as the UniFi panic handler returning 200 OK, on a longer clock. Upstream silent failure, downstream visible spike.

19Thread V · Queue exposure

Applicants stuck waiting on approval emails

Nineteen applicants are currently waiting for approval; three of them have been waiting longer than three weeks. Approval emails route through the same affected Brevo sender. The door system hasn't failed the people on the "waiting for approval" list only because it hasn't let them in yet. It's the smallest finding in the chapter and the easiest to reach: a same-day approval SLA plus a verified-reputation sender retires the risk.

Read forward: the smallest fix in the chapter, the most visible win.

The engineer's investigation made the pipe legible. The warehouse priced it. Both numbers point the same direction.

What both investigations share

Both investigations describe the same function, applied to different surfaces of the business.

The pattern

Both threads followed the same path. A misleading surface symptom (“ensure your device is connected to the internet” on a door reader, an April cancellation spike on a billing dashboard) hid a quiet upstream cause: a panic handler returning 200 OK, or a sending-reputation collapse two months earlier. Each fix needed a map of every actor and what they own (Ubiquiti, the cred-server, and the EFG and UNVR firmware on one side; Stripe, the Brevo sending domain, dunning, and billing-ops on the other), because each fix was a multi-team handoff. And each investigation left infrastructure behind: six standing procedures and a bug-class taxonomy for UniFi, a reusable warehouse-query method for BI. The infrastructure built alongside the investigation is the deliverable.
